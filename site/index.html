It works, it interoperates, and it is not ready for real identities yet.

decID lets a person prove “a trusted office verified my identity”, or just “I am over 18”, without handing over their name or birth date. This page tests that claim three ways. Verdict

Could this be used in the real world?

The protocol: yes works and interoperates

The credential format, signatures, selective disclosure and on-chain checks do what they claim. Independent libraries with no shared code accept the credentials: the OpenWallet Foundation SD-JWT library, viem, Python’s cryptography, and the official W3C schema and context. 53 attacks run against a live stack were all stopped by the specific defense meant to stop them.

The deployment: not as it stands prototype

The issuer is simulated. It approves synthetic applications and checks no real documents. Keys live in an environment file, not a hardware module. The chain is a local test network. Nothing has been audited, and there is no trust framework, legal basis or wallet-standard integration. Each of these is buildable, but each is real work.

Problems found by testing, and fixed

  1. this evaluationTwo app instances could leave a revoked credential active.Two servers sharing one issuer key revoked at the same time and raced for the transaction nonce. 2 of 4 revocations failed, under a misleading “chain unreachable” error. Now a cross-process lease serializes them: 8 of 8 succeed (npm run test:multi-instance, in CI).
  2. this evaluationJSON-LD-aware verifiers rejected the credential.The W3C VC v2 context defines iss, sub, iat and exp but not jti, and it has no fallback vocabulary. The credential’s own context now defines jti, and a test expands every credential in safe mode against the vendored W3C context.
  3. earlierThe rate limiter could be bypassed with a spoofed header.Found by the red team: rotating X-Forwarded-For passed 10 of 10 requests over the limit. The header is now trusted only behind a declared proxy.
  4. earlierVerification took 51 ms, of which 3 ms was cryptography.Found by the benchmark: a 10 ms default delay in ethers’ RPC batching, paid three times in a row. It now takes about 20 ms.

        How it works

        Three parties, one chain, and very little on it

        The issuer checks an application, then signs a credential that says “identity verified” plus three optional claims. Each optional claim is hidden behind a salted hash. The holder’s wallet address is the credential’s subject.

        The holder registers a random-looking commitment to that credential on-chain, from their own wallet. The chain never sees the credential, the claims or the salt.

        A verifier sends a one-time challenge. The holder signs it with their wallet and reveals only the claims the verifier asked for. The verifier checks the issuer’s signature, the disclosures, the wallet signature and the commitment, then reads the live registry. Revocation and expiry show up there.

        The gate is a smart contract that lets a wallet through only while its credential is valid on-chain.
